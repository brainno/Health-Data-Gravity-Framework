Health Data Gravity Framework

Whoever holds and controls health data
shapes the healthcare landscape around them.

A framework for understanding how healthcare systems are structured, where they are heading as data ownership shifts from institutions to individuals, and what role primary care plays in what comes next.

Youngin Lee, MD · Shailendra Prasad, MD · Esther M Johnston, MD · Timothy J Beebe, PhD · Paul B Batalden, MD
Viewpoint prepared for submission to JMIR · 2026

01 — Why Now

Personal health data is everywhere.
It belongs to no one in particular.

AI and digital health tools are arriving faster than the ideas needed to guide preparation for this new data environment. Three structural gaps explain why.

Fragmented Records

A patient's health story is split across dozens of silos — GP, hospital, pharmacy, apps. No single actor holds a complete picture.

No Motivation to Engage

Previous personal health record platforms failed not because of technology, but incentives. Patients had no tangible reason to aggregate and maintain their own records.

Data Value Captured Elsewhere

When patient data flows to pharma or insurers, the economic value bypasses the very people who generated it — patients and primary care professionals.

02 — The Core Idea

From forces to geometry

The concept of data gravity draws on a central insight from general relativity: massive objects do not pull each other through a hidden force — they bend the space around them. Applied to healthcare, any entity that holds health data bends the accessibility landscape around itself, drawing clinical resources, specialists, and patients inward by the shape of the field.

A heavy ball on a stretched rubber sheet sags the sheet downward; a marble rolled nearby simply follows the slope inward — no external force required.

New to general relativity? A short primer ↗

Data Mass

The combined weight of the data an entity holds. Volume — the sheer amount of clinically relevant data captured — is the primary driver. Clinical usefulness matters too: a well-structured longitudinal record bends the landscape more than an equally large but fragmentary one.

Accessibility A(e) & Usability U(e)

Two dimensions determine where data mass exerts its pull. A(e) measures whether an entity can reach and act on data — legal rights, interoperability, interface design. U(e) measures whether it can turn access into meaningful action — literacy, agency, tool quality. Crucially, both can rise simultaneously for patients and institutions: data sovereignty is not a zero-sum competition for control, it is the expansion of capacity.

Policy Field & Technology Efficiency

A policy background field — data-ownership laws, interoperability standards, AI regulations — acts uniformly across the entire landscape. Well-designed policy lifts the whole landscape; poorly designed policy reinforces institutional dominance. Technology efficiency, increasingly AI-driven, determines how well raw data converts into meaningful care.

How Data Mass Grows and Shrinks

It grows when patients capture more signals from their lives — wearables, home diagnostics, patient-reported outcomes — and when infrastructure lets them hold, move, and grant access to that data. It shrinks when data is walled inside institutional silos, when consent is withdrawn or allowed to lapse, or when access rights are lost during care transitions.

08 — What Must Be True

Three priorities for a fairer tilt

The direction is not automatic. Three conditions determine whether the shift toward Sovereign Gravity produces a fairer system.

01

Closing the digital gap

Digital health tools are spreading faster than the systems needed to ensure they benefit everyone. Primary care professionals serve as guides for patients not yet comfortable with digital tools — helping them act on data signals they cannot access alone. The most digitally excluded patients are also those who most need the Holistic Companion's human skills.

02

Redesigning how care is paid for unfunded orbit

Pay-per-visit models do not reward the ongoing work the HDGF envisions. Compensating professionals for integrating data, maintaining a long-term view, and preventing illness would better reflect primary care's value. Without this realignment, health systems risk what we term an unfunded orbit: data sovereignty moves toward patients, but payment structures still pull resources toward institutional care.

03

Technology and trust

Patients sharing data expands both accessibility and usability, but fears about unfair AI and commercial misuse remain real barriers. Federated learning — training models without patient data leaving the institutions or devices that hold it — is one technical solution, but the most durable foundation for trust is the ongoing human relationship of the Holistic Companion.

10 — Try It

HDGF Agent: the framework as a working prototype

An AI agent built on one shared record, with two views. The patient controls what is shared, and the agent can only read what consent allows. The physician's agent sees only what the patient has chosen to share.

Patient mode

Understand and use your own data

A personal agent that reads the whole record on the patient's behalf.

  • Explains lab results and trends in plain language
  • Helps prepare questions for the next GP visit
  • Sets each section to Share, AI summary only, or Private, and answers physicians' access requests
  • Finds nearby specialists and clinical trials that may fit
Physician mode

Understand the patient more deeply

A clinical co-author assistant that works only within the patient's consent.

  • Chooses from a patient list; summarises each shared record and flags trends, using only the summary where that is all the patient shares
  • Points to what the data cannot show: private context, emotion, the embodied encounter
  • Suggests open questions and requests access to unshared data with a stated reason
